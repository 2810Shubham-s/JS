ABSTRACT

Bihar, one of the most flood-prone states in India, experiences widespread devastation annually due to the overflowing of rivers Ganga, Kosi, Gandak, and Bagmati. According to the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA), approximately 73% of Bihar's total geographical area falls within flood-risk zones, displacing millions of citizens each monsoon season and placing enormous strain on emergency response systems.

Despite the existence of a large volunteer base and numerous active NGOs, emergency coordination in Bihar remains fragmented, reactive, and largely paper-based. The absence of a unified digital platform results in delayed response times, duplication of relief efforts, lack of transparency in fund utilisation, and inadequate volunteer management.

This synopsis presents Bihar Sahayata, a full-stack, mobile-first Disaster Relief and Emergency Coordination Platform purpose-built for the state of Bihar. The platform bridges the critical operational gap between distress-stricken citizens, field-deployed volunteers, and disaster management authorities through a suite of tightly integrated digital tools.

The system architecture encompasses a production-grade Node.js REST API (50 endpoints) secured by role-based access control, a serverless PostgreSQL database (19 tables), and a cross-platform React Native mobile application serving three distinct user roles: Citizens, Volunteers, and Administrators. Core functionalities include GPS-based SOS panic alerts using the Haversine geolocation algorithm, structured issue reporting and lifecycle tracking, volunteer verification and team management, official disaster declaration workflows, and transparent crowdfunding campaign management.

The platform is engineered to operate reliably on low-bandwidth mobile connections — a critical requirement for disaster-affected zones — and is deployed via Docker containerisation on Fly.io cloud infrastructure. Bihar Sahayata represents a complete, end-to-end demonstration of modern full-stack development principles, encompassing database design, API security, geospatial computation, cloud deployment, and human-centred mobile interface design within the academic framework of the BCA curriculum at Patna University.

Keywords: Disaster Relief · Emergency Coordination · React Native · Node.js · Geolocation · Haversine Formula · PostgreSQL · Role-Based Access Control · Volunteer Management · Bihar · Crowdfunding · REST API

CHAPTER 1

Introduction
Context, Background, and Motivation

Introduction

1.1  The Disaster Landscape of Bihar

Bihar occupies a geographically vulnerable position in the eastern Indo-Gangetic plain, drained by some of India's most volatile river systems. The rivers Ganga, Kosi — often called the 'River of Sorrow' — Gandak, and Bagmati routinely breach their banks during the monsoon season, inundating vast swathes of agricultural and residential land. The Kosi river alone has shifted its course by more than 100 kilometres over the past two centuries, making it one of the most unpredictable rivers on the subcontinent.
According to the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA), approximately 73 per cent of Bihar's total geographical area is classified as flood-prone, affecting nearly 76 of its 101 sub-divisions. In the 2019 floods alone, more than 8.1 million people were displaced across 13 districts, and the economic losses were estimated at over ₹1,200 crore. These numbers underscore not merely a climatic challenge, but a systemic governance and coordination crisis.

1.2  The Coordination Gap

Despite the well-established nature of Bihar's flood crisis, emergency response continues to be hampered by a fundamental coordination deficit. Volunteer groups, NGOs, government relief agencies, and affected citizens operate in silos, with information flowing through informal telephone chains, social media posts, and physical notice boards. The absence of a unified, real-time communication infrastructure means that:
Citizens in distress are unable to reach verified help quickly.
Volunteers often respond to the same incident while other affected areas remain unaddressed.
Relief funds reach intended beneficiaries only after significant delays and with limited transparency.
Authorities lack a consolidated operational picture of active incidents across districts.
This fragmentation is not a problem of intent or resources — it is a problem of missing digital infrastructure.

1.3  Bihar Sahayata — The Platform

Bihar Sahayata is conceived as a unified, mobile-first digital platform that directly addresses the coordination gap described above. The name — translating to 'Bihar Help' or 'Bihar Assistance' — reflects the platform's core mission: to be a dependable, always-available bridge between those who need help and those who can provide it.
The platform provides three distinct, role-differentiated interfaces that work in concert across a single unified backend:

4.2  Issue Resolution State Machine

The lifecycle of every reported incident in Bihar Sahayata follows a formally defined state machine. This ensures that issues cannot skip states arbitrarily, that responsibility is always assigned, and that audit trails are maintained for accountability. The state machine is enforced at the API middleware layer, not merely in the front-end application.
 The system enforces strict role-based access control to ensure that only authorized entities can initiate or approve specific state transitions. It maintains a single source of truth for each incident, preventing duplicate or conflicting actions across different actors. All state changes are recorded in real time, enabling detailed post-incident analysis and evaluation of response efficiency. This structured approach enhances consistency, reliability, and operational discipline, especially during high-pressure disaster scenarios.

4. Haversine Geolocation Algorithm

The selection of nearby volunteers for SOS alerts is the most algorithmically critical operation in the Bihar Sahayata platform. The system uses the Haversine Formula — a standard in spherical trigonometry — to compute great-circle distances between the incident's GPS coordinates and each registered volunteer's location stored in the database.
The Haversine Formula is chosen over Euclidean distance calculation for the following technical reasons:
Accuracy: Euclidean distance assumes a flat plane, which introduces significant error over distances greater than a few kilometres on the Earth's curved surface.
Numerical Stability: The Haversine formulation remains numerically stable for both very small distances (adjacent streets) and large distances (cross-district searches).
Database Integration: The computation can be performed directly within PostgreSQL SQL queries, eliminating the need to transfer large result sets to the application layer for filtering.

The formula, as applied within the platform's SQL queries, is expressed as follows:

d = 2r · arcsin( √[ sin²((φ₂−φ₁)/2) + cosφ₁ · cosφ₂ · sin²((λ₂−λ₁)/2) ] )

where            r = 6,371 km            |            φ = latitude (radians)            |            λ = longitude (radians)
■            Figure 4.3 — Haversine Formula for great-circle distance computation

Volunteer search radii are calibrated to operational realities: SOS Panic Alerts trigger a 20 km search radius (maximum urgency), while High-Severity Issue Reports trigger a 15 km radius. The formula's parameters are computed within PostgreSQL's native mathematical functions, with latitude and longitude stored as DECIMAL(10, 7) fields to ensure sub-metre precision.
